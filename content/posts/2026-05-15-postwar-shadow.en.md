# Postwar Shadows: Constitutional Revision and Historical Revisionism

On August 15, 1945, Japan announced its unconditional surrender. This was followed by the Allied occupation and transformation of Japan, a democratization process, and a "Peace Constitution" that remains controversial to this day. Seventy years later, the debate over whether to revise this constitution is not merely about Japan's domestic affairs — it sits at the heart of historical reconciliation and security dynamics across East Asia.

This is the third article in the "Reflections on Japanese Militarism" series. Here we examine the political博弈 around Article 9 and the systematic penetration of historical revisionism into Japanese society.

---

## Part 1: Article 9 of the "Peace Constitution" — Blessing or Curse?

### The Birth and Meaning of Article 9

The Constitution of Japan, promulgated in 1947, contains Article 9:

> "Aspiring sincerely to an international peace based on order and justice, the Japanese people forever renounce war as a sovereign right of the nation and the threat or use of force as means of settling international disputes.  
> To accomplish the aim of the preceding paragraph, land, sea, and air forces, as well as other war potential, will never be maintained. The right of belligerency of the state will not be recognized."

This provision, hailed as "a first in human history," was drafted directly under the主导 of the General Headquarters of the Allied Powers (GHQ). Japanese Diet members did not participate in its drafting; the Yoshida government accepted it almost entirely as presented.

Article 9 legally disarmed Japan, entrusting its security entirely to America's nuclear umbrella (the "San Francisco System").

### The "Self-Defense Right" Dispute: Judicial Interpretations Around Article 9

However, the "Peace Constitution" was never monolithic. The Japanese government gradually constructed a complex theory of "self-defense rights" through a series of judicial interpretations:

- **Self-defense rights acknowledged**: In 1954, Japan established the "Self-Defense Forces" (SDF), circumventing Article 9's prohibition on "war potential" through the doctrine of "minimum necessary self-defense"
- **Three Principles on Arms Exports**: Although restricted, Japan's defense spending and weapons procurement continued expanding throughout the Cold War
- **2014 relaxation of "Three Principles on Arms Exports"**: The Abe administration replaced the old arms export ban with "Three Principles on Transfer of Defense Equipment," opening the door to Japanese military exports

Japan's Supreme Court has never ruled Article 9 unconstitutional, nor has it ever provided a clear interpretation of its constitutionality. This legal ambiguity has provided operating space for successive governments' attempts to revise the constitution.

## Part 2: The Revision Movement — A Century-Long Obsession of the Right

### From Yoshida Shigeru to Abe Shinzō

In the early postwar period, Prime Minister Yoshida Shigeru favored the "light armament, economic priority" path ("Yoshida Doctrine"), focusing on economic development while relying on U.S. security guarantees. This approach was criticized within his own party during the height of the Cold War as "excessively weak."

Entering the 21st century, with China's rise and North Korean nuclear tests, calls for Japan's "normal statehood" grew louder domestically. In 2007, Japan's Defense Agency was formally upgraded to a full Ministry of Defense, paving the way for the constitutional revision agenda.

### 2023 Constitutional Reform Initiatives and the "Defense Capabilities" Clause

In 2023, Japan's ruling coalition of the Liberal Democratic Party (LDP) and Kōmeitō formally submitted a constitutional amendment proposal to the Diet, seeking to add a "defense capabilities" clause to Article 9, explicitly recognizing the SDF's legal existence and incorporating "state emergency power" provisions.

Supporters argue that in the context of a rapidly deteriorating security environment, constitutional revision is a necessary step toward Japan's "normal statehood." Critics warn that this opens the floodgates to militarist revival and severely damages Japan's relations with neighbors like China and South Korea.

## Part 3: Yasukuni Shrine: Honoring Class-A War Criminals

### History and Nature of Yasukuni Shrine

Yasukuni Shrine, located on Kudanzaka in Chiyoda, Tokyo, was established in 1869 to enshrine the spirits of military personnel who died serving the Emperor from the Meiji Restoration onward. After World War II, it was seized and reorganized as a non-state entity, but in 1978, the spirits of 14 Class-A war criminals including Tōjō Hideki and Matsui Iwane were secretly enshrined alongside others.

### The Controversy of Official Visits

Since 1985, Japanese Prime Minister Nakasone Yasuhiro began official visits to Yasukuni Shrine in his official capacity, provoking strong protests from China, South Korea, and other nations. Since then, successive Japanese prime ministers have visited in "private capacity," attempting to avoid diplomatic controversy — but this "compromise" has never been accepted by victimized nations.

The fundamental reason China and South Korea strongly oppose prime ministerial visits to Yasukuni is that enshrining Class-A war criminals there equates those who planned and executed the wars of aggression to "national martyrs who died for their country" — this itself is a glorification of aggressive war history.

### 2023: New Developments in Memorial Facilities for Japan's War Dead

The Kishida Fumio government held the national memorial ceremony for war victims at the Tokyo Budōkan in August 2023, continuing the practice of many years and attempting to use a more "neutral" facility to circumvent Yasukuni's political controversies. However, this arrangement has been criticized as "changing the soup without changing the ingredients."

## Part 4: The Textbook Controversy — Contesting Historical Narrative

### "Bōkoku Kyōkasho" and the "Jiyū Shuppan Historiography"

The Japanese textbook controversy has a long history, with its core contradiction being: Should history textbooks "accurately reflect historical facts" or should they "maintain Japan's positive image"?

Right-wing publishing houses, represented by "Jiyū Shuppan" (Liberty Publishing), have long published textbooks that use "advance into" rather than "invade" to describe the 1937 Nanjing Incident. These textbooks have less than 1% market share in Japan, but their political influence far exceeds their market proportion — through pressuring schools, lobbying education boards, and other means, they influence regional education policies.

### 2023 New Curriculum Standards: Progress and Regression

In 2022, Japan's Ministry of Education, Culture, Sports, Science and Technology (MEXT) announced new junior high school curriculum guidelines (学習指導要領), partially restoring content on "postwar responsibility" in history courses, but simultaneously deleting references to the "forcible recruitment of comfort women" from previous versions.

Organizations including Amnesty International and Human Rights Watch strongly criticized these changes, pointing out that the Japanese government is systematically erasing records of war atrocities from the educational system.

## Part 5: Historical Revisionism — A Systematic Denial Project

### What Is Historical Revisionism?

In academic discourse, historical revisionism refers to the selective reinterpretation of historical events with the intent to deny or downplay conclusions from established historical consensus that are unfavorable to a particular group or nation.

In the Japanese context, historical revisionism primarily manifests as systematic denial or minimization of:

- **The Nanking Massacre**: "No systematic massacre occurred; casualty figures have been exaggerated"
- **Unit 731**: "Insufficient evidence for human experimentation"
- **The "Comfort Women" system**: "Not coercive; was commercial activity"
- **The nature of aggression**: "The advance was to liberate Asia, not an invasion"

### "Tsukurukai" and "Nippon Kaigi"

The "Atarashii Rekishi Kyōkasho o Tsukurukai" (Association for the Creation of New History Textbooks), established in 1997, is the core organization of Japan's historical revisionist movement. Its members include prominent scholars, politicians, and media figures who, through publishing textbooks, publishing commentaries, and lobbying schools and education boards, systematically advance a historical narrative of "glorifying Japanese history and denying war crimes."

According to research by Japanese scholars, this organization has close personnel and financial ties to "Nippon Kaigi" (Japan Conference), Japan's largest right-wing political organization, which maintains substantial political influence within both the Abe and Kishida governments.

## Conclusion

From Article 9 of the Peace Constitution to Yasukuni Shrine, from textbooks to the constitutional revision movement, domestic Japanese historical revisionism has formed a systematic project spanning education, culture, and politics. Its purpose is not to uncover historical truth, but to clear the historical-moral obstacles to Japan's "normalization" and military relaxation through reshaping collective memory.

However, history's scars do not disappear through denial. Denial of war crimes not only wounds the feelings of directly victimized peoples in China, Korea, and other nations — it also deprives Japanese society itself of the opportunity to face history honestly and move toward genuine reconciliation.

True reconciliation begins with an honest confrontation with history.
